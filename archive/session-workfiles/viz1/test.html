The budget gauge

A 10×10 grid of rounded boxes that fills from the bottom like liquid. This board draws the open questions around it (what the liquid means, the scale, many categories, the Home gauge, the states, what a tap reveals, how it moves) for six kinds of student, not one.

Your references, and what is taken from each

Waffle grids

Taken: rounded boxes, filled from the bottom, quiet grey for the empty ones, one short line of words under the grid, dark ground, a single fill colour.Changed: the slide's grids are 8 wide with a ragged top row. Trickle's is always 10×10, and the top edge is the thing section B has to settle.Left out: the big percentage. Home shows no number, and a category shows words first.

Apple Fitness rings

Tried: a ring as the overall Home gauge (D2), nested rings for the top three categories (C4), the week strip of day minis (D3, D4).Taken into the tap layer: the "750/500" pattern, one exact figure against its target, becomes "₹140 left of ₹400" after a tap (F2).Caution: that screen shows a strip, three rings, a stat and a bar chart at once. It is the opposite of one visualisation per screen, and its rings rely on three memorised colours, which Trickle has already rejected for categories.

The student pool

Every option below is drawn for all six. They differ in money, number of categories, period, how long they have used the app, and where in the period they are.

A

What does the liquid mean?

Money left, which drains down as you spend, or money spent, which rises. Same students, same numbers, drawn both ways.

A1 LeftThe start of a period is a full, bright grid, and the worst state is a dark, quiet one. Nothing lights up when things go badly. A payment takes boxes away, which is the loss the pay screen (visualisation 2) wants to make felt. It matches a fuel gauge and the question students already ask, "how much do I have".
A2 SpentGautham on Tuesday is a nearly blank screen at his best moment. Vaishak, almost out of money, gets the fullest and brightest grid of the six. A payment adds boxes, so paying looks like gaining. Going over would mean more liquid than the grid holds.
Where rising does belongSmall buys adding up (visualisation 6) is an accumulation, and rising suits that. Keeping the budget gauge as "left" and accumulation as its own form avoids one shape meaning two opposite things.

Leaning A1, money left. It is the gentler of the two (the bad state is the quiet one) and it carries friction (boxes leave when you pay).

B

The scale, and the top edge

With 100 boxes always, one box is the budget divided by 100. That is ₹60 for a ₹6,000 budget and ₹6.52 for a ₹652 one. The alternative rounds the box to a clean value and leaves the unused boxes dotted. Separately: is the top of the liquid an even level across the row, or does it fill box by box?

Top edge, close up

What one box is worth, across the pool

B1 Exact, budget ÷ 100The grid always is the budget: full means full, for everyone. The cost is fussy values (₹6.52, ₹9.20, 68 paise for a ₹68 subscription budget). Nobody counts 100 boxes, so the label gives a sense of size, not arithmetic, and an odd value does little harm.
B2 Round box, unused boxes dottedClean values, but Gautham's untouched ₹652 looks two-thirds full and Nishad's ₹6,000 loses 40 boxes. There are now two kinds of empty box (spent, and never there), and the whole is no longer the grid. ₹652 at ₹10 a box also needs 65.2 boxes, so the 66th is only a fifth real.
A third way: round the budget, not the boxIf budgets are set in steps of ₹100, one box is always a whole number of rupees and B1 never looks fussy. Odd budgets then only appear when the app derives one, and those can read "1 box is about ₹6.50".
B3 Box by boxOne partly filled box at most, with a liquid level inside it. A ₹20 chai at ₹4 a box is exactly five boxes leaving, which lines up with the pay screen's crumbling squares. The even level (B1) is the more literal liquid, but it leaves ten half-filled boxes on the top row and the scale can no longer be checked by eye.

Found while drawing: at ₹25,000 one box is ₹250, so a ₹20 chai moves the monthly gauge by less than a tenth of a box. Honest, but there is no visible drop and no friction at that level. The category gauge (₹60 a box for food) and the week gauge do show it.

Leaning B1 with budgets set in ₹100 steps, written "1 box = ₹X" beside a small box glyph, filled box by box (B3). The fill question is close: the even level scores one point lower only because it cannot be checked against the scale.

C

Many categories: 2, 3, 4, 7, 12, 18

Four ways to get from "all my categories" to one gauge, each tested against one visualisation per screen and against Harsh's 18.

C1 One grid per screen, snap scrollThe purest reading of the rule, and perfect for Vaishak, Gautham and Tarun (2 to 4 screens). At 18 it is 17 swipes to reach the last one and there is no way to find Gym except by scrolling past everything.
C2a Index with tiny barsFast to scan, but 18 small bars side by side are 18 small gauges, in a second form. This is more than one visualisation on a screen, and it invites comparing categories with different budgets by bar length.
C2b Index with words onlyNames and one or two words ("nearly empty", "most left"). It is a list, not a chart, so the rule holds. Tap opens the full grid; swiping inside moves to the next. Works the same at 2 and at 18, and custom names (Gym, Bike EMI, Gifts) are as clear as defaults.
C3 One grid, a band per categoryReadable at 2 to 4. At 7 half the bands are too thin to name, at 12 and 18 almost all are. It needs a way to tell bands apart (two alternating tones here, since colour-coded categories were rejected) and it cannot show a category that has gone over: Harsh's Eating out simply disappears.
C4 Nested rings, top 3 + othersA second form for the same information, so "grid = budget" stops being consistent. Without three memorised colours the rings need a legend to say which is which. "Others" hides 15 of Harsh's 18, including the ones he made himself.

Found while drawing: rent, an EMI, a gym fee or a phone plan go from full to empty in a single payment. Sorted "emptiest first" they sit at the top looking like trouble when they are simply paid. They are drawn here with the word "paid" and sorted last. Whether fixed bills get a gauge at all may belong to visualisation 8 (subscriptions coming up).

Leaning C2b into C1: a words-only index, tap for the grid, snap scroll between grids. With four categories or fewer the index can be skipped and the app opens straight on the first grid.

D

The overall gauge on Home

One sentence and one gauge, no ₹. Grid or ring, each with and without a week strip of day minis.

D1 GridThe same form as every category screen, so it is learned once. The sentence does the reading; the grid backs it up.
D2 RingReads about as fast and looks friendlier, but it is a second form for the same idea, and a ring has no boxes, so the logged rule "the scale is always shown" has nothing to attach to.
D3 / D4 With the week stripSeven minis plus the main gauge is eight gauges on one screen. It breaks the rule as written, and the minis are too small to read as levels at phone size. As plain day letters with today marked, the strip is navigation and does not count. As a picture of the week day by day, it is its own visualisation, one scroll down (it overlaps visualisation 9, this week vs last).

Found while drawing, two conflicts: (1) "the scale is always shown" meets "no ₹ on Home". D1 writes "1 box = 1% of this week" as a stand-in; the alternatives are to show the ₹ scale anyway or to reveal it on tap. (2) Home is "this week", but Nishad, Harsh and Vaishak run on a month or on irregular money. Their week has to be worked out from what is left, and it can disagree with the month: Vaishak's month is nearly empty while his week reads "most of it left".

Leaning D1, grid, with no day strip on Home. The week day by day becomes the next screen down.

E

States

Full, half, nearly empty, empty, three ways to draw "gone over", and last week's level as a ghost line. Each state is shown on the student it happens to; pick one student above to see all eight on the same budget.

EmptyA dim grid and the word "empty". No red, no warning icon, no number. People stop looking at money they expect to be bad (the ostrich effect), so the empty screen has to be one a student is willing to open again tomorrow.
Gone over, E5 boxes below the floorWith "left", going over is going below zero, so the extra sits under the grid as hatched boxes. It shows how far, but only for small overs: Harsh's ₹3,500 dinner on a ₹2,000 budget is 95 boxes under, so it has to be capped and the drawing stops being exact.
Gone over, E6 amber floor lineThe grid is empty and its floor turns amber, with the words "gone over". Works for any size, stays calm, and the amount waits for the tap. It says less at a glance.
Gone over, E7 hatched inside the gridFills the grid from the top with "owed" boxes. It looks busy, reads as a second liquid, and breaks at more than 100%.
E8 Ghost lineA dashed line where the level was at this point last week. Useful, but it is a second mark to decode on the glance layer. It may fit better after the tap, or be left to visualisation 9.

Leaning E6 at a glance (amber floor line and words), with the amount on tap, and the ghost line on the tap layer, not the glance.

F

Progressive disclosure

Glance, then tap, then detail. Rupees appear only after the first tap.

F1 GlanceGrid, name, words, scale. No amount.
F2 TapThe exact figure against the budget (the Apple "750/500" pattern), and a pace: what that is per day for the days left. The grid does not move, so the number is read against the picture.
F3 DetailThe grid shrinks to a corner and the transactions take the screen. A list is not a second visualisation.

Found while drawing: the per-day pace is kind when there is money (Tarun, "about ₹35 a day") and bleak when there is not (Yash, "about ₹30 a day" for a weekend). It may need to switch to words below some level.

G

Motion

Two moments: the liquid settling when the screen opens, and the level dropping when a payment lands.

  • Settle on open, about 2 s: the surface sloshes once and flattens to a faint meniscus. It never loops, so a resting screen is still.
  • Payment, about 1.1 s: the level drops with a small overshoot, the boxes that just left stay as a pale ghost for a second, then fade. No red, no shake.
  • Reduced motion: the level changes without animation.
  • Box-by-box variant: the same drop, but the boxes empty one after another from the top row, right to left, about 40 ms apart.

Static frames

Scores

1 to 5 on each test, 5 best. These are judgements from the drawings above, not test results.

The whole must be visibleGarcia-Retamero and Cokely's work on icon arrays: people read a part correctly when the whole it belongs to is drawn. The 10×10 does this by construction. Dotted "unused" boxes (B2) and a ring with no boxes (D2) weaken it.
100 boxes are a level, not a countKay et al. 2016 found people can count and reason with about 20 marks. 100 is well past that, so nobody will count them. The grid reads as a height, and the "1 box = ₹X" label gives a feel for size. This is why an odd box value is tolerable, and why a single clear top edge matters.
The ostrich effectPeople avoid checking finances they expect to be bad (Karlsson, Loewenstein and Seppi 2009). It matches Tarun's finding that a bad number on Home makes people close the app. Empty and gone-over are drawn to be calm for this reason.

Decisions for Tarun

1. What does the liquid mean?

  1. Money left. It drains as you spend (A1).
  2. Money spent. It rises (A2).

2. How is the scale kept readable?

  1. Exact, whatever it comes to: "1 box = ₹6.52" (B1).
  2. Exact, and budgets are set in ₹100 steps so a box is whole rupees.
  3. Round the box (₹5, ₹10, ₹25, ₹100) and dot the unused boxes (B2).

3. What is the top edge of the liquid?

  1. An even level across the whole row, with a meniscus (B1).
  2. Box by box, with a liquid level inside the last box (B3).
  3. Box by box, whole boxes only, rounded to the nearest box.

4. How does a student with many categories reach a gauge?

  1. Snap scroll only, emptiest first (C1).
  2. Words-only index, tap for the grid, snap scroll inside (C2b + C1).
  3. Index with tiny bars (C2a). Breaks one-per-screen.
  4. One layered grid (C3) or rings (C4).
Also: do fixed bills (rent, EMI, gym) get a gauge, the word "paid", or move to visualisation 8?

5. What is the Home gauge?

  1. The 10×10 grid, no day strip (D1).
  2. A ring (D2).
  3. Either, with a week strip of day minis (D3, D4).
And the scale on Home: hidden until tap, "1 box = 1% of this week", or the ₹ scale shown even though Home has no other ₹?

6. How does "gone over" look?

  1. Hatched boxes below the floor, capped at two rows (E5).
  2. Amber floor line and the words "gone over"; amount on tap (E6).
  3. Hatched boxes inside the grid from the top (E7).
And the ghost line for last week: on the glance, on tap, or not here at all?

Still open from the drawing and not in the six: what the "whole" is for a student with no budget (Tarun-type in week one), and how Home's week is worked out for monthly and irregular money.
