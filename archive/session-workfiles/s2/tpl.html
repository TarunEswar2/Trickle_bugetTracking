Representing money

Fifteen ways to draw the same student month, tested on ten real scenarios. Explorations only. Tarun decides.

“1 dot = ₹100 is easy, but the problem comes with them combining into a line and a box. The biggest problem with not combining is larger amounts become harder to represent.”

“₹6,000, ₹600 for food, ₹500 for tea — all these numbers are too much.” — Tarun

The problem has three parts. Dots that merge into lines and boxes add a second and third shape to learn. Dots that never merge explode at ₹45,000 (450 marks, far past the ~20 a person can count at a glance; Kay 2016). Raw numbers everywhere are what students already tune out. Colour-coded categories add a key to memorise. What we keep: income splits into spending and savings, unspent money is saved, and progressive disclosure is how detail arrives.

Scenarios use the 6-month seed: ₹25 chai, ₹350 meal, ₹9,000 income (₹2,000 save, ₹7,000 spend), week ₹1,750 with ₹1,050 left, month ₹7,000 with ₹2,600 left, Food ₹2,800 · Travel ₹1,200 · Fun ₹1,000 · Study ₹600 · Subs ₹548, a ₹45,000 laptop goal, a ₹14,000 goal with ₹8,110 saved, four chais, and night spending. Filled = money still yours; outline = spent; dashed = not yet saved; ochre = savings.

{{MATRIX}}

Research behind the criteria. Kay et al. 2016: about 20 countable marks per glance; 100+ loses the benefit. Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995: counts (“3 of 10 days”) beat percentages, which hurts the waffle. Barrio 2016 / Riederer 2018: one perspective line in familiar units helps recall; small multipliers work best, so “≈ 1,800 chais” fails while “≈ 6 months of food” works. Olafsson & Pagel 2018 (ostrich effect): bad-news screens are avoided, so Home stays word-led. Garcia-Retamero 2010: show the whole, so spent stays as outlines. Neurath: repeat a unit, never enlarge it, which rules out gauges and big circles for big sums.

Top 4

S1 · Fixed dots, changing scale

  • Pro: one shape forever; dots never merge, which is Tarun’s concern.
  • Pro: every view stays under 20 marks; ₹45,000 = 9 dots of ₹5,000.
  • Con: the same dot means ₹100 or ₹1,000, so the unit must always be written.
  • Con: comparing week and month views needs the label.

S7 · Relative words

  • Pro: no number on Home; calmest (ostrich effect).
  • Pro: carries patterns (“nights cost more”).
  • Con: no proportion by itself; vague without a picture.
  • Con: weak friction at pay.

S8 · Label-first ranked bars

  • Pro: names on bars, so no colour key.
  • Pro: aligned lengths are the most accurate read (Cleveland & McGill).
  • Con: shows ₹, so it belongs in a tab, not Home.
  • Con: weak at ₹25.

S3 · Days of spending

  • Pro: uses Tarun’s “₹100–150 a day” finding; “5 days left” is felt.
  • Pro: scales: ₹45,000 ≈ 7½ months.
  • Con: depends on a “usual day” the student must accept.
  • Con: two meanings of “day” (calendar vs money).

Recommended hybrid (S15)

Home: one sentence in words (S7) over a week of ₹100 dots (S1): 17½ dots, filled = left, outline = spent. Under 20 marks; no ₹ figure. Pay moment: the dots the payment takes turn dashed before you confirm (friction, Soman 2001). Chai = ¼ dot; four chais fill one dot. Spending tab: label-first ranked bars by category (S8). Month dots switch to ₹1,000, with “1 dot = ₹1,000” written on screen. Savings / big goals: scaled dots (₹5,000) plus one days or months line (S3): “≈ 6 months of saving”. Detail: exact ₹, own equivalents (S12), and the hour strip for night spending (S11).

Why: dots never merge into lines or boxes, and large amounts are handled by stating the unit, not by new shapes. Words protect Home from the big number. Category names sit on the marks, so there is nothing to memorise. Rejected: gauges and waffles fail ₹45,000; notes, tallies and count labels lose honest proportion at scale.
